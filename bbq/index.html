BBQ and safety-trained large language models

BBQ is the most widely used bias benchmark for language models. Built before safety-trained models became widespread, it now meets models that abstain, which its score cannot see and its aggregate cannot place. Pick a model, then step through the tiers.

How BBQ works

BBQ has 58,492 multiple-choice questions across eleven social categories. Each gives a short context about two people, a question, and three choices: a member of a stereotyped group, the other person, and "unknown". Try one below.

The tiers

  1. Tier 0: the four card numbers plus the worst-case group summary.
  2. Tier 1: five fields per category.
  3. Tier 2: every group with intervals, sample sizes, and accuracy cost.
  4. Tier 3: the templates behind each group.
  5. The setup section publishes prompt format, decoding, parsing, and model identifiers.

Measures. DSUR: unknown rate on disambiguated questions. DSSR: stereotype-aligned answers on ambiguous questions. DSA: accuracy when the group's individual is correct. CAG: negative minus non-negative accuracy.

Provenance. Rates pool each model's five trials. Intervals are template-clustered bootstrap intervals: the study's own for the three main models, computed here and marked "site" for the others.

Card values are from the published Claude system cards.

Is abstention protective?

Where abstention falls

If abstention protected a group, a model that refuses one question about the group would refuse the others. The study found that refusals track how a question is worded, not who it is about. The attribution test

Share of abstained responses contradicted by the model's own answers elsewhere, at three strictnesses: the twin question, the same type and group, any template about the group.
Inverted: the share where the harmless twin is the one refused.

What this does and does not show. The answers these models commit to are clean; wrong-person errors stay below 1%. What fails is the claim that withholding protects anyone: the withheld answers fall without regard to the stakes, and the bias score cannot register any of it.

The formula and its blind spots

The bias score

sDIS = 2 ·nstereonstereo + ncounter− 1

"Unknown" is not in the formula, so a model that abstains on one group and answers on another can still score zero.

What aggregation hides

Most groups appear on a handful of templates. Even a fully stereotyped group moves the overall score by under 2 points.

What these measures cannot fix

They inherit BBQ's limits: culturally specific stereotypes, some groups on a single template, no same-group comparisons, and likely presence in training data. Equal abstention across groups also gives zero spread while hiding uniform suppression.
